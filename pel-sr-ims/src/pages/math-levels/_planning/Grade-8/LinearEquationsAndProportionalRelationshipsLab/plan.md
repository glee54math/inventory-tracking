# LinearEquationsAndProportionalRelationshipsLab

**Proposed slot:** MM3 — provisional, confirm at build time per project convention. See the MM3-contention flag in Open questions (shared across all four grade-8 labs in this batch, and with grade 7's own MM3 proposal).
**Grade:** 8
**Standards covered:** 8.EE.5 (graph proportional relationships, interpret the unit rate as the slope of the graph, compare two different proportional relationships represented in different ways), 8.EE.6 (use similar triangles to explain why the slope m is the same between any two distinct points on a non-vertical line in the coordinate plane, derive the equation y = mx for a line through the origin and y = mx + b for a line intercepting the vertical axis at b), 8.EE.7a (solve linear equations in one variable, including cases with one solution, infinitely many solutions, or no solutions — show which case by transforming the given equation into simpler forms until an equivalent equation of the form x = a, a = a, or a = b results), 8.EE.7b (solve linear equations with rational number coefficients, including equations whose solutions require expanding expressions using the distributive property and collecting like terms).
**Relationship to existing labs:** Builds on grade 6's `OneVariableEquationsAndInequalitiesLab` (planned in a separate batch for the Grade-6 folder, covering 6.EE.5-8's foundational one-step equation solving) and grade 7's `ExpressionsAndEquationsLab` (planned in a separate batch for the Grade-7 folder, covering 7.EE.1/7.EE.3-4's multi-step equations with rational coefficients, distributive property, and px+q=r / p(x+q)=r forms). This lab assumes a student arriving here can already isolate a variable through multiple steps, apply the distributive property, and collect like terms comfortably — it does NOT re-teach basic equation-solving mechanics. What's genuinely new here: (1) classifying an equation by its SOLUTION COUNT (exactly one / infinitely many / none) rather than assuming every equation has exactly one answer, and (2) connecting equation-solving to graphing — unit rate as slope, similar triangles explaining why slope is constant, and deriving y = mx / y = mx + b as equation FORMS rather than just solving for an unknown. Neither prerequisite lab covers either of these.
**Audience framing:** Built for a student seeing this content for the first time, regardless of the standard's posted grade level. The three-solution-count idea (one / infinite / none) should be grounded in graphing BEFORE being treated as an abstract algebra rule — a student can SEE two lines cross once, lie exactly on top of each other, or run parallel and never touch, and that visual should come first, with the algebraic "simplify until you reach x=a, a=a, or a=b" procedure introduced as the symbolic way to predict what the graph will show, not the other way around.

## Units

### Unit 1: Proportional Relationships, Unit Rate, and Slope
- **Goal:** Graph a proportional relationship and see that the steepness of the line is exactly the unit rate, no matter how the relationship was first described.
- **Standard:** 8.EE.5
- **Buckets:**
  - Graphing a proportional relationship from a given unit rate/equation (e.g. y = 3x) and reading the slope directly off the graph as rise/run between two plotted points
  - Given a table of values for a proportional relationship, finding the unit rate (slope) from the table without graphing first, then confirming it matches the graphed slope
  - Comparing two proportional relationships represented in DIFFERENT forms (e.g. one as a graph, one as a table, or one as a verbal description like "costs $4 per item") to determine which has the greater rate — the standard's own explicit "represented in different ways" comparison requirement
  - A real-world context (e.g. two different cars' constant speeds, two phone plans' per-minute rates) where the student must extract the unit rate from a messier/noisier representation (a word problem or a partially-filled table) rather than a clean equation, to confirm the skill transfers beyond tidy symbolic inputs
- **Requires intro (CFU gate):** Yes — connecting "slope" (a new graphing term) to "unit rate" (a term carried over from grade 6/7 ratio work) is the core new idea, and a common misconception is treating slope as just "a number on the graph" disconnected from the rate-of-change meaning it already has in context; CFU should include a question asking the student to state, in their own words, why the unit rate and the slope are the same number for a proportional relationship.
- **Memorization fact (periodic re-check candidate):** No — re-derivable each time from rise-over-run reasoning; the connection to unit rate is conceptual, not a fact to look up.

### Unit 2: Why Slope Is Constant — Similar Triangles and Deriving y = mx (+ b)
- **Goal:** Use similar right triangles drawn under a line to see WHY the slope between any two points on that line is always the same number, then use that idea to write the line's equation.
- **Standard:** 8.EE.6
- **Buckets:**
  - Given a line through the origin with two right triangles drawn beneath two different pairs of points on it, verify the triangles are similar (matching angle markings or side-ratio check) and that the rise/run ratio comes out equal for both — the direct, concrete version of the "why" before any equation is written
  - Deriving y = mx for a line through the origin, given its slope, and verifying the equation against a plotted point not used to find the slope
  - Deriving y = mx + b for a line that crosses the vertical axis away from the origin, identifying b as the starting value (y-intercept) and m as the same constant slope from the similar-triangles reasoning, verified against a point
  - A "same slope, different line" discrimination case: two lines with identical slope but different y-intercepts, requiring the student to recognize the slope-via-similar-triangles argument applies to EACH line independently (same m, different b) rather than assuming equal slope means the same line
- **Requires intro (CFU gate):** Yes — the similar-triangles justification is a genuinely new, named argument (not just "slope is rise over run" as a computational rule) and directly addresses a documented 8th-grade target idea; CFU should include a vocabulary question distinguishing "the slope is the same between ANY two points on the line" from "all lines with the same slope are the same line" — two closely related but distinct true statements that are easy to conflate into one overclaim.
- **Memorization fact (periodic re-check candidate):** No — this is a one-time geometric justification meant to be understood and re-derivable by picture, not a fact to memorize independent of the reasoning.

### Unit 3: One Solution, Infinitely Many, or No Solution
- **Goal:** Solve a linear equation by simplifying both sides, and recognize from the result whether the equation has exactly one solution, every number as a solution, or no solution at all.
- **Standard:** 8.EE.7a
- **Buckets:**
  - **Exactly one solution:** an equation that simplifies to a single value, x = a (e.g. 3x + 5 = 2x + 9 → x = 4) — the baseline case, verified by substituting back into the ORIGINAL equation, not the simplified form
  - **Infinitely many solutions:** an equation where both sides simplify to the exact same expression, leaving a true statement like a = a (e.g. 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6 → 6 = 6) — paired explicitly with a graphing interpretation (both sides describe the same line) so the student sees WHY every x works, not just that the algebra canceled out
  - **No solution:** an equation where the variable terms cancel but the constants don't match, leaving a false statement like a = b (e.g. 2x + 3 = 2x + 7 → 3 = 7) — paired with the graphing interpretation (two parallel lines that never meet), and explicitly distinguished from "x = 0," since a documented misconception is reading "no solution" as "the solution is zero" rather than "there is no number that works"
  - A disguised case where the equation LOOKS like it should have one solution (different coefficients on each side at first glance) but the distributive property or like-term collection reveals one of the three cases above only after simplifying — so the solution-count can't be guessed by surface appearance alone
- **Requires intro (CFU gate):** Yes — this is the unit's core named misconception territory: students assume every linear equation has exactly one solution and either panic or guess when terms cancel out, and separately confuse "no solution" with "x = 0." CFU must include at least one question presenting both an infinite-solutions and a no-solution equation side by side and asking the student to identify which is which and WHY (not just produce the simplified result), per the gate's vocabulary/conflation requirements.
- **Memorization fact (periodic re-check candidate):** No — the three outcomes are recognized by RUNNING the simplification each time, not memorized as a lookup; the skill is the process, not a retained fact.

### Unit 4: Solving Linear Equations with Rational Coefficients
- **Goal:** Solve linear equations whose numbers are fractions or decimals, using the distributive property and combining like terms, building directly on the solution-count skill from Unit 3.
- **Standard:** 8.EE.7b
- **Buckets:**
  - An equation with fractional coefficients solved directly (e.g. (1/2)x + 3 = 7), including the option of clearing denominators by multiplying through, as an efficiency strategy worth showing explicitly
  - An equation with decimal coefficients solved directly (e.g. 0.4x - 1.2 = 2), kept as a separate bucket from fractions since decimal arithmetic errors (misplaced decimal points) are a different failure mode than fraction arithmetic errors
  - An equation requiring the distributive property to expand BEFORE collecting like terms, with at least one rational (fraction or decimal) coefficient involved (e.g. (2/3)(x + 6) = 10), combining both new skills in one problem rather than testing them in isolation
  - A rational-coefficient equation that lands in one of Unit 3's infinite-solutions or no-solution cases (e.g. (1/2)(4x + 6) = 2x + 3), confirming the solution-count classification skill transfers to messier coefficients and isn't only ever demonstrated with clean whole numbers
- **Requires intro (CFU gate):** No — this unit is primarily extended practice applying Unit 3's classification skill and prior grades' distributive-property/like-terms mechanics to messier (rational) numbers; no genuinely new concept is introduced here that warrants its own gated walkthrough.
- **Memorization fact (periodic re-check candidate):** No — pure extended practice, re-derivable via the same procedure as whole-number cases.

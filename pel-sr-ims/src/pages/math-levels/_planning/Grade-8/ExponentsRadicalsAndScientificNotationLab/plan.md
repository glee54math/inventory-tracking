# ExponentsRadicalsAndScientificNotationLab

**Proposed slot:** MM3 — provisional, confirm at build time per project convention. See the MM3-contention flag in Open questions (shared across all four grade-8 labs in this batch, and with grade 7's own MM3 proposal).
**Grade:** 8
**Standards covered:** 8.EE.1 (know and apply the properties of integer exponents to generate equivalent numerical expressions), 8.EE.2 (use square root and cube root symbols to represent solutions to equations of the form x² = p and x³ = p where p is a positive rational number, evaluate square roots of small perfect squares and cube roots of small perfect cubes, know that √2 is irrational), 8.EE.3 (use numbers expressed in the form of a single digit times an integer power of 10 to estimate very large or very small quantities, and to express how many times as much one is as the other), 8.EE.4 (perform operations with numbers expressed in scientific notation, including problems where both decimal and scientific notation are used, use scientific notation and choose units of appropriate size for measurements of very large or very small quantities, interpret scientific notation generated by technology).
**Relationship to existing labs:** Extends the already-built `MM1_ExponentsAndExpressionsLab`'s (grade 6) "Exponents" unit, which establishes exponent NOTATION itself but only for positive whole-number exponents (e.g. 3⁴ = 3×3×3×3) used to generate equivalent numerical expressions under 6.EE.1. This lab assumes that foundation is already solid — a student arriving here should already read/write exponent notation and compute a positive-whole-number power comfortably — and extends it to INTEGER exponents, including negative and zero exponents, plus the properties that govern combining them (product/quotient/power rules), none of which `MM1_ExponentsAndExpressionsLab` covers. It also introduces radicals (square/cube roots) and scientific notation, both brand-new representations with no prior lab coverage anywhere in the codebase.
**Audience framing:** Built for a student seeing this content for the first time, regardless of the standard's posted grade level. Negative exponents are the single most counter-intuitive new idea here (something gets SMALLER as the exponent that looks like it should make it bigger goes negative) — ground this in an extended number pattern the student can verify by division (…, 2³=8, 2²=4, 2¹=2, 2⁰=1, 2⁻¹=1/2, …, each step dividing by 2) before stating the "negative exponent = reciprocal" rule abstractly. Likewise, scientific notation should be grounded in a real large/small-quantity context (distances in space, sizes of cells) before being treated as pure notation manipulation.

## Units

### Unit 1: Integer Exponent Rules
- **Goal:** Discover the patterns for multiplying and dividing powers with the same base, raising a power to a power, and what negative and zero exponents actually mean, by extending the whole-number exponent pattern the student already knows.
- **Standard:** 8.EE.1
- **Buckets:**
  - Multiplying two powers with the same base (product rule, e.g. 3² × 3⁴ = 3⁶), including verifying it by expanding both sides as repeated multiplication
  - Dividing two powers with the same base (quotient rule, e.g. 5⁶ ÷ 5² = 5⁴), including the case where the quotient rule produces a NEGATIVE exponent (e.g. 5² ÷ 5⁶ = 5⁻⁴), forcing the negative-exponent meaning to connect immediately to a procedure the student just used, not just an isolated definition
  - Raising a power to a power (e.g. (2³)² = 2⁶) and distributing an exponent over a product (e.g. (2×3)² = 2²×3²), kept as a distinct bucket since students commonly conflate "multiply the exponents" with "add the exponents" from the product rule
  - Evaluating/simplifying an expression that mixes a zero exponent and a negative exponent in the same problem (e.g. 4⁰ × 2⁻³), the hardest-tier case, since both "anything to the zero power is 1" and "negative exponent means reciprocal" must be applied correctly in the same expression rather than in isolation
- **Requires intro (CFU gate):** Yes — negative and zero exponents are brand-new, counter-intuitive ideas with a well-documented misconception (assuming a negative exponent makes the result negative, rather than a reciprocal); CFU must include a true/false generalization question (e.g. "is 2⁻³ the same as -2³? Why or why not, and what IS 2⁻³?") that makes students articulate the distinction rather than just compute one example.
- **Memorization fact (periodic re-check candidate):** Yes — the exponent rules themselves (product, quotient, power-of-a-power, zero exponent, negative exponent = reciprocal) are a small, fixed rule set used as a building block throughout the rest of this lab and future algebra; worth a `reintroEverySolved` periodic re-check rather than one-time proof, since fluency here is foundational and easy to let slip.

### Unit 2: Square Roots, Cube Roots, and Why √2 Is Irrational
- **Goal:** Use the square-root and cube-root symbols to find the number that solves x² = p or x³ = p, and understand why not every square root comes out as a nice whole number.
- **Standard:** 8.EE.2
- **Buckets:**
  - Evaluating the square root of a small perfect square (e.g. √36 = 6), including recognizing both the positive root as "the" answer presented and that squaring it checks the answer
  - Evaluating the cube root of a small perfect cube (e.g. ∛27 = 3), kept as a separate bucket from square roots since the "undo" relationship (cubing, not squaring) is a different operation students must track correctly rather than defaulting to square-root habits
  - Solving an equation of the form x² = p or x³ = p for a given positive rational p, read as "what number, squared/cubed, gives me this," not just "evaluate this root symbol" — connects the symbol back to the equation it solves per the standard's own framing
  - Identifying that a given square root (e.g. √2, √10) is NOT a perfect square and therefore has no nice whole-number value, explicitly including √2 by name and connecting back to `IrrationalNumbersAndApproximationLab`'s irrational-number concept if that lab is built first (and otherwise introducing "irrational" fresh here for √2 specifically, per the standard's own explicit callout)
- **Requires intro (CFU gate):** Yes — "√2 is irrational" is explicitly named in the standard as a required fact, not left implicit, and students commonly confuse "square root" with "half" or assume every square root simplifies to a clean decimal; CFU should include a vocabulary question distinguishing "squaring a number" from "taking its square root" in the same question, since these are commonly conflated as inverse operations that get mixed up under time pressure.
- **Memorization fact (periodic re-check candidate):** Yes — the small perfect-squares table (1-144) and perfect-cubes table (1-125, say) used to evaluate roots instantly rather than by trial-and-error are worth periodic re-checking as a standalone recall fact set, since fast recognition of these speeds up everything downstream in this unit and the Pythagorean-theorem work in `PythagoreanTheoremAndVolumeLab`.

### Unit 3: Estimating with Powers of 10
- **Goal:** Write a very large or very small number as a single digit times a power of 10, and use that form to quickly compare how many times bigger or smaller one quantity is than another.
- **Standard:** 8.EE.3
- **Buckets:**
  - Converting a large whole number (e.g. 350,000) into single-digit-times-power-of-10 estimate form (≈ 3 × 10⁵)
  - Converting a small decimal (e.g. 0.00041) into the same form (≈ 4 × 10⁻⁴), kept distinct from the large-number bucket since tracking a negative exponent for a small number is a different direction of reasoning than a positive exponent for a large one
  - Comparing two quantities already given in this form and stating "how many times as much" one is as the other (e.g. 6×10⁸ is how many times 3×10⁵?), which requires dividing both the digit part and the power-of-10 part correctly, not just eyeballing the exponents
  - A real-world estimation context (population sizes, distances, or similarly scaled quantities) where the student must first round/estimate a messy given number into single-digit-times-power-of-10 form before comparing, rather than being handed already-clean numbers
- **Requires intro (CFU gate):** Yes — "single digit times a power of 10" is a specific, narrower form than general scientific notation (which Unit 4 covers), and students commonly skip the rounding-to-one-digit step or get the sign of the exponent backwards for small numbers; CFU should explicitly ask the student to identify why a stated estimate form is or isn't valid (e.g. is 35 × 10⁴ a correctly single-digit form? why not?).
- **Memorization fact (periodic re-check candidate):** No — the estimation/rounding process is re-derivable each time from place value; no fixed fact distinct from Unit 1's exponent rules and Unit 4's notation conventions.

### Unit 4: Scientific Notation — Operations and Real Units
- **Goal:** Write numbers in full scientific notation, convert between scientific and standard decimal notation, perform operations on numbers in scientific notation, and choose sensible units for very large or very small measurements.
- **Standard:** 8.EE.4
- **Buckets:**
  - Converting a standard decimal number to proper scientific notation (coefficient between 1 and 10, times a power of 10) and back, in both directions, for both large and small numbers
  - Multiplying two numbers in scientific notation (multiply coefficients, add exponents), including the case where the coefficient product is 10 or greater and must be renormalized (e.g. (4×10⁵)(3×10³) = 12×10⁸ = 1.2×10⁹) — the single most commonly skipped step in this unit
  - Adding or subtracting two numbers in scientific notation with DIFFERENT exponents (must align powers of 10 before combining, directly analogous to aligning decimal places), kept distinct from multiplication since the alignment requirement is new and easy to skip when exponents don't already match
  - A mixed decimal/scientific-notation problem (per the standard's own explicit "problems where both decimal and scientific notation are used" language) where one value is given in standard decimal form and the other in scientific notation, requiring the student to convert one before combining them, plus a "choose the appropriate unit" real-world problem (e.g. should a virus's size be reported in meters or in a smaller unit, given its scientific-notation measurement) and a technology-display interpretation case (e.g. reading a calculator's "3.2E7" or "3.2×10^7" display format)
- **Requires intro (CFU gate):** Yes — proper scientific notation has a specific structural rule (coefficient strictly between 1 and 10) that's easy to violate without noticing, and the renormalization step after multiplication is a real, specific, commonly-skipped mistake; CFU should include a true/false check on whether a given expression (e.g. 12×10⁸) is already in proper scientific notation, forcing the student to name the rule being violated rather than just produce a correct answer from scratch.
- **Memorization fact (periodic re-check candidate):** No — the conversion and operation procedures are re-derivable each time from place-value/exponent-rule reasoning (which Unit 1 already covers as its own memorization set); scientific notation itself is a notation convention applied via those rules, not a separate fact to memorize.

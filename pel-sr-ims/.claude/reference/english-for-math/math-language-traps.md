# Math-language traps (all grades)

Wording that makes students get math wrong even when they know the math.
Check every lab's text against this list. The grade files say *when* each
word arrives; this file says *why* it's dangerous.

## 1. Everyday words with a different math meaning
A student hears the everyday meaning first. Teach the math meaning with a
picture, and avoid using the everyday meaning in the same lab.

| Word | Everyday | Math | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| left | side; remaining | how many remain | K–1 |
| same / equal | alike in some way | exactly the same amount | K–1 |
| fourth, quarter | place in line; coin; time | one of 4 equal parts | 1–2 |
| even / odd | flat, fair, "even though" / strange | divisible by 2 or not | 2 |
| difference | how things aren't alike | how far apart two numbers are | 2 |
| foot, yard | body part; backyard | units of length | 2 |
| times | occasions | multiply | 3 |
| product | thing to buy | answer to multiplying | 3 |
| round | circle-shaped | nearest ten/hundred | 3 |
| area, volume | a place; loudness | square units; cubic units | 3, 5 |
| table | furniture | rows of data | 3+ |
| right, degree | correct / side; temperature | 90° angle; angle unit | 4 |
| multiple, prime | many; best | multiple of a number; only 1 and itself | 4 |
| base, power, origin, plot | baseball; strength; beginning; story | prism base; exponent; (0,0); place a point | 5 |
| mean, range, rate, interest, similar, function | unkind; stove; speed; curiosity; kind of alike; party | statistics; ratio; same shape; rule | 6–8 |

## 2. Homophones the voice can't tell apart
In read-aloud labs the student *hears* the word. These sound identical:
*sum/some, whole/hole, two/to/too, four/for, eight/ate, one/won, cents/sense,
pair/pear, plane/plain, weigh/way, pi/pie, sell/cell.*
The sentence and picture must make the meaning clear on their own. "Find the
sum" heard as "find the some" means nothing.

## 3. Word order is not the order you do it
The student does the operations in the order the words come, and gets it
backwards.

| Words | Means | Not |
|---|---|---|
| subtract 3 from 10 / take 3 away from 10 | 10 − 3 | 3 − 10 |
| 5 less than 12 | 12 − 5 | 5 − 12 |
| 12 divided into 3 groups / divided by 3 | 12 ÷ 3 | |
| 3 divided into 12 | 12 ÷ 3 | 3 ÷ 12 |
| the quotient of a and b | a ÷ b | |
| add 8 and 7, then double it | 2 × (8 + 7) | 8 + 7 × 2 |

Use the wording where order matches the math until the reversed form is the
thing being taught.

## 4. Comparison language
The hardest structure in elementary word problems.
- **More / fewer / less.** *Fewer* is for things you count; *less* is for
  amounts. Young children rarely say *fewer*; teach it.
- **Relational sentences.** "Ann has 3 more than Ben" describes how two
  amounts relate; it isn't an event. Students grab "more" and add. When the
  words point the wrong way ("Ann has 3 more than Ben. Ann has 7. How many
  does Ben have?"), most students add and get it wrong. CCSS has students
  meet that version in 1st grade but expects mastery only in 2nd; the
  version where the words point the right way is expected in 1st.
- **How many more / how many fewer** means the gap between two amounts, not
  an event. 1st grade (1.OA, 1.MD.4).
- **Times as many** (4th) vs. **more than** (1st). Put them side by side once
  both are taught.
- **Comparatives with negatives** (6th): "colder," "deeper," "lower" mean a
  smaller number.

## 5. Small words that carry the math
*of, each, every, per, than, by, at least, at most, no more than, between,
both, either, all, some, in all, altogether, left over.*
- "Between 3 and 7" — are 3 and 7 included? Say so.
- "Each" vs. "in all" decides group size vs. total.
- "Of" means multiply only from 4th–5th; earlier it's just "a part of."

## 6. Grammar that raises the reading load
In order of how much it hurts (Abedi & Lord, 2001):
1. Long noun phrases ("the total number of red and blue marbles").
2. Passive voice ("24 cookies were shared by 4 friends").
3. Conditional clauses that come first ("If each box holds…").
4. Relative clauses ("the boy who has fewer").
5. Negatives in the question ("Which is NOT…", "not more than").
6. Pronouns that point back two sentences.
7. Unfamiliar names and contexts (see below).

## 7. Background knowledge the story assumes
Sports scoring, allowance, coins before 2nd grade, "a dozen," tips and tax,
miles per gallon, holidays, seasons, snow. A student who doesn't know the
setting is solving two problems. Use settings any child knows (school,
food, toys, animals, family), or explain the setting in one sentence.
Names should be easy to decode (or, in read-aloud labs, easy for the voice
to say), and pronouns must match them.

## 8. Symbols and text the voice must say
- "a-b" ranges → "a to b". "vs." → "and". "→" → "then".
- "?" in the middle of a sentence → "the box with the question mark".
- "−" is *minus* between numbers and *negative* in front of one.
- "1/4" → "one fourth" (not "one slash four"). "0.4" → "four tenths" while
  place value is being taught.
- "3²" → "3 squared"; "10³" → "10 cubed" or "10 to the third power".
- "30s" → "thirties". "a.m." → "A M".
- Teen numbers: say them, but show the ten and the ones.

## 9. Keyword teaching
"'Left' means subtract," "'in all' means add," "'more' means add." Each one
breaks on the first story that doesn't follow it (all of section 4 above).
Teach the *situation* (a picture of what happens), never a keyword list.

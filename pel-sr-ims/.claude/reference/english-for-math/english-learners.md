# English learners

About 1 in 5 California students is classified as an English learner, and
the most common home language by far is Spanish. These students know far
more math than their English can show. Every guideline in the grade files
helps them; these are the extra points.

## English level matters as much as grade
The California ELD Standards describe three levels. A 3rd grader at the
Emerging level needs the K–1 limits for language, even with 3rd-grade
math.

| ELD level | Language they can handle in a math problem |
|---|---|
| **Emerging** | Single words and short phrases, lots of pictures, the voice; key words shown with a picture. |
| **Expanding** | Short sentences with familiar patterns; new words need an example. |
| **Bridging** | Most grade-level English; idioms and dense sentences still slow them down. |

## What helps
- **Pictures that carry the meaning on their own,** the same picture style
  every time.
- **The same sentence pattern every time** ("There are __. __ more come. How
  many now?"). Patterns let a student read the math rather than the English.
- **Spanish cognates** make many math words easier: *number/número,
  equal/igual, sum/suma, total/total, difference/diferencia,
  fraction/fracción, divide/dividir, multiply/multiplicar,
  triangle/triángulo, perimeter/perímetro, area/área,
  equivalent/equivalente, symmetry/simetría.* Prefer the cognate when there's
  a choice ("equal" over "the same as" once both are taught).
- **The voice.** Spoken English is usually ahead of reading English for
  English learners too.

## What hurts
- **Phrasal verbs and idioms:** *take away, add up, give away, pick out,
  left over, in all, altogether, figure out, ballpark.* The meaning isn't
  in the separate words. Teach the few the lab needs; avoid the rest.
  When a lab teaches a phrasal verb in one unit (e.g. *go into* in Unit 2),
  earlier units use a word students already know instead (*multiple*).
- **Several words for one idea in the same unit** (*add, plus, sum, total,
  in all, altogether, combined*). Pick one; add the others one at a time.
- **Idioms in feedback:** "You nailed it!", "Piece of cake!", "Hang in
  there." Use plain praise: "Yes! That's right."
- **Assumed cultural settings** (see math-language-traps.md §7).

## Math conventions that differ between countries
Students who started school elsewhere (or whose parents did) may know a
different notation:
- **The decimal comma:** many countries write 3,5 for three and a half and
  1.000 for one thousand. A student may read 1,000 as "one point zero."
- **Long division** is laid out differently in much of Latin America and
  Europe. The math is the same; the picture isn't.
- **Spanish teen numbers** (*once, doce, trece, catorce, quince*) are also
  irregular, and the regular pattern starts at *dieciséis* ("ten and six"),
  which actually helps place value. English *eleven–fifteen* is harder.
- **Dates** (day/month), **"billion"** in older usage (a million million),
  and the 24-hour clock.

## Quick check for an English-learner pass
1. Could a student who knows only the taught math words, and none of the
   story's other words, still solve it from the picture?
2. Is each math idea named by one word in this unit?
3. Any idiom, phrasal verb or cultural setting that isn't needed?
4. Do numbers use one format everywhere (commas for thousands, a period
   for decimals), and are they spoken clearly?
